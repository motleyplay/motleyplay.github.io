How to Solve Logic Grid Puzzles: From Basic Elimination to Murder Mystery Deduction

By Larry Henry | Published Nov 12, 2026 | Updated Nov 17, 2026 | 2967 words | min read

You have nine clues, four guests, a dead lord on the conservatory floor, and a staircase of ninety-six empty squares. You read every clue once. You drop a handful of ✕s into the grid. Not one clue hands you a dot. Then you sit back and look at a board where nothing else seems to move.

So you read the clues again. Same nine sentences. Same handful of ✕s. You start eyeing two squares and wondering which one is “probably” right.

You haven’t run out of clues. You’ve run out of first-pass information. The first trip through the clues only sets the boundaries; the actual solve happens when you squeeze ordered ladders, lock pairs of items together, and copy what you know from one part of the grid into another. Each of those moves is mechanical once you know what to look for, and none of them involve guessing.

How a Staircase Logic Grid Is Built

A logic grid puzzle hands you several categories (suspects, rooms, weapons, times), each with the same number of items. Every item pairs with exactly one item in every other category. One guest, one room, one object, one time. Nobody shares.

The grid records every possible pairing as a small square, and it’s folded into an L-shaped staircase so each pair of categories appears exactly once:

                  Room            Object
              +-------------+-------------+
  Suspect     |  Block A    |  Block B    |
  (4 rows)    |  Suspect ×  |  Suspect ×  |
              |  Room       |  Object     |
              +-------------+-------------+
  Object      |  Block C    |
  (4 rows)    |  Object ×   |
              |  Room       |
              +-------------+

There’s no Suspect × Suspect block, because that question is meaningless, and no Room × Suspect block, because Block A already asks it. With C categories you get C × (C − 1) ÷ 2 blocks:

  • 3 categories of 4 items: 3 blocks, 48 squares (Death on the Night Mail)
  • 4 categories of 4 items: 6 blocks, 96 squares (Death at Ravenscroft Manor)
  • 4 categories of 5 items: 6 blocks, 150 squares (The Last Sitting at Hollowmere)

The thick lines between blocks matter more than they look. How Logic Grid Matrices Work covers the geometry in full; the short version follows.

The Three Marking Rules

  1. ✕ rules out a pairing. “Dr. Pryce was not seen at 9:45 PM” is one ✕.
  2. ● confirms a pairing, and clears its row and column inside that block. Inside one 4 × 4 block, each row and each column holds exactly one ●. Placing a ● therefore crosses out the other 3 squares in its row and the other 3 in its column: six ✕s for free.
  3. Last square standing. When a row or column inside a block has every square but one crossed out, the survivor is a ●.

Here is rule 2 triggering rule 3 in a generic Suspect × Room block (X = ✕, O = ●, . = blank):

Before                          After one ● at (B, Room 2)

      R1  R2  R3  R4                  R1  R2  R3  R4
  A   .   .   X   .               A   .   X   X   .
  B   .   .   .   .       →       B   X   O   X   X
  C   X   .   .   X               C   X   X   .   X
  D   .   .   .   .               D   .   X   .   .

One dot placed six ✕s, and row C now has three of them. Its last open square, Room 3, is a ●, which fires rule 2 again.

The trap: those auto-✕s stop at the thick border. Confirming that Suspect B was in Room 2 says nothing yet about B’s row in the Suspect × Object block next door. Getting information across that border is a separate step, and it’s where stalled puzzles get unstuck.

The Six Clue Types and What Each One Marks

Every clue in a standard logic grid falls into one of six patterns. Each pattern produces a predictable set of marks the moment you read it, whether or not you know where anybody is yet.

Direct Clues: “Was” and “Was Not”

A direct positive clue states a match outright: “The actor who dressed on the first floor first entered in Act IV” (Curtain Call at the Gaslight Theatre). One ●, six auto-✕s.

A direct negative clue rules out one pairing: “Dr. Pryce was not seen at 9:45 PM.” One ✕. Weak on its own, but negatives stack. Ravenscroft gives you a second one (“Dr. Pryce was not seen at 9:30 PM”), and suddenly Dr. Pryce’s time row is down to two squares.

Either Clues: Cross Out Everything Else

“The passenger in Compartment 3 was either Mr. Quill or Miss Ingram” (Death on the Night Mail).

You don’t know which one. You don’t need to. You know the other two passengers were not in Compartment 3:

                    Comp 1  Comp 2  Comp 3  Comp 4
Mrs. Fairweather      .       .       X       .
Captain Royle         .       .       X       .
Miss Ingram           .       .       .       .
Mr. Quill             .       .       .       .

An either clue gives you n − 2 ✕s on the first read, where n is the number of items per category: two on a 4 × 4 grid, three on a 5 × 5.

Pair Clues: “Of A and B, One Was X and the Other Was Y”

These sound vague and do more work than any other clue type. Take Clue 8 from Death at Ravenscroft Manor: “Of the guest in the Wine Cellar and the guest in the Billiard Room, one was Miss Thorne and the other was Dr. Pryce.”

Read it as four either clues at once. Miss Thorne was in one of those two rooms. So was Dr. Pryce. The Wine Cellar held one of those two guests. So did the Billiard Room. Mark all four:

               Library  Conservatory  Billiard Rm  Wine Cellar
Colonel Ashby     .          .            X            X
Lady Vane         .          .            X            X
Dr. Pryce         X          X            .            .
Miss Thorne       X          X            .            .

Eight ✕s from one sentence, and the block has split into two locked 2 × 2 boxes. Colonel Ashby and Lady Vane now share the Library and Conservatory between them, so a single ✕ in either box solves both of its squares. When two pair clues share a name, they often hand you three ●s at once; Mastering “Either/Or” and “One of These Two” Clues walks through that move on a 5 × 5 grid.

Ordered Clues: “Earlier Than” and “Exactly Two Before”

Times, compartments, floors, and acts are ladders: their items have a fixed order. Comparison clues on a ladder carry hidden ✕s at the ends.

“The guest holding the letter opener was seen earlier than the guest in the Library” (Ravenscroft, Clue 4) gives you three ✕s:

less:  Letter Opener earlier than Library

               9:00   9:15   9:30   9:45
Letter Opener   .      .      .      X     <- can't be last
Library         X      .      .      .     <- can't be first

Plus: Letter Opener ✕ Library  (nobody is seen earlier than themselves)

An exact-gap clue cuts deeper. “Mr. Quill was two compartments nearer the engine than Miss Ingram” (Night Mail, Clue 5) only fits two ways on a four-compartment carriage: (1, 3) or (2, 4).

gap = 2:  Mr. Quill two compartments nearer than Miss Ingram

              Comp 1  Comp 2  Comp 3  Comp 4
Mr. Quill       .       .       X       X
Miss Ingram     X       X       .       .

Now look back at the either clue above. Compartment 3 was Mr. Quill or Miss Ingram, and Mr. Quill just got crossed out of it. Miss Ingram is in Compartment 3, which forces the (1, 3) window and puts Mr. Quill in Compartment 1. Two clues that named no confirmed facts produced two ●s. Cracking Ordered Clues covers ladder squeezing in detail.

Which Clues to Mark First

Now that you’ve seen each type in action, here is the order that clears the most squares fastest on a first pass:

Clue typeSounds likeFirst-pass yield (4 × 4 block)
Direct positive“A was B”1 ● + 6 auto-✕s
Pair“Of A and B, one was X and the other Y”8 ✕s (12 on a 5 × 5)
Either“A was either X or Y”2 ✕s (3 on a 5 × 5)
Exact gap“A was two before B”2 ✕s per step of gap, +1 if A and B are different categories
Inequality“A was earlier than B”2 ✕s, +1 if A and B are different categories
Direct negative“A was not B”1 ✕

One more rule for the clue list: strike out a direct clue once its marks are down, but leave ladder clues live. An “earlier than” clue keeps producing ✕s every time either item loses a rung.

Breaking Mid-Game Stalls With Cross-Referencing

Back to that frozen board. Every clue is marked, no row has a single open square, and rereading isn’t helping. The missing marks are sitting in the grid. Clues scatter their information across three or six separate blocks, and the grid doesn’t connect those blocks for you.

Copy Marks Across a Confirmed ●

When a ● links two items, they’re the same person. Every ✕ one of them has against a third category belongs to the other one too.

Gaslight Theatre shows this right away. Clue 4 confirms First Floor = Act IV. Now look at what the other clues say about each:

  • Clue 1: the first-floor actor was not Mr. Bramwell.
  • Clue 2: Miss Lark entered one act before Mr. Bramwell, so Miss Lark wasn’t in Act IV.
  • Clue 6: Signor Valli entered earlier than somebody, so Signor Valli wasn’t in Act IV.

Copy the Act IV ✕s onto First Floor and the First Floor ✕ onto Act IV:

Actor × Floor (First Floor)        Actor × Entrance (Act IV)
Mr. Bramwell    X  (Clue 1)        Mr. Bramwell    X  (copied)
Miss Lark       X  (copied)        Miss Lark       X  (Clue 2)
Mrs. Penrose    .                  Mrs. Penrose    .
Signor Valli    X  (copied)        Signor Valli    X  (Clue 6)

Both columns now show last square standing: Mrs. Penrose dressed on the first floor and entered in Act IV. No clue says that directly.

Negative Transfer

You can also pass a ✕ through a ● into a block where you haven’t confirmed anything yet. If the Major was in the Library (●), and the guest in the Library wasn’t holding the rope (✕), then the Major wasn’t holding the rope (✕). It reads as obvious in a sentence. On paper the three facts sit in three different blocks, and you will miss this one more often than any other move.

Transitive Cross-Referencing turns these moves into a three-step audit you can run any time the board stalls.

Practical Example: The Poisoned Decanter at Briarwood Club

This case is built for this article, so it spoils nothing on the playable mysteries. It’s small enough to finish in your head and uses every move above.

The steward of the Briarwood Club found cyanide in the port decanter after the evening toast. Four members sat by the fire in chairs numbered 1 to 4, Chair 1 nearest the fireplace, and each drank one thing. Whoever drank the port is your poisoner.

  1. Of Lord Vance and Dr. Finch, one drank brandy and the other drank sherry.
  2. Lord Vance sat two chairs nearer the fireplace than Dr. Finch.
  3. Dr. Finch sat nearer the fireplace than the member drinking whisky.
  4. General Sterling did not sit in Chair 4.
  5. The member in Chair 1 did not drink sherry.

Pass 1: Pair and Direct Clues

Clue 1 is a pair clue: eight ✕s in Member × Glass, locking Lord Vance and Dr. Finch to {Brandy, Sherry} and the other two members to {Port, Whisky}. Clues 4 and 5 add one ✕ each.

                 | C1 C2 C3 C4 | Br Po Sh Wh
-----------------+-------------+-------------
Lord Vance       |  .  .  .  . |  .  X  .  X
General Sterling |  .  .  .  X |  X  .  X  .
Dr. Finch        |  .  .  .  . |  .  X  .  X
Judge Blackwood  |  .  .  .  . |  X  .  X  .
-----------------+-------------+
Brandy           |  .  .  .  . |
Port             |  .  .  .  . |
Sherry           |  X  .  .  . |
Whisky           |  .  .  .  . |

Pass 2: Squeeze the Chair Ladder

Clue 2 is a gap of 2 on four chairs, so the only windows are (1, 3) and (2, 4). Lord Vance loses Chairs 3 and 4; Dr. Finch loses Chairs 1 and 2.

Clue 3 is an inequality. Dr. Finch can’t be in the last chair (✕ Chair 4), and the whisky drinker can’t be in the first (✕ Whisky at Chair 1).

Now Dr. Finch’s chair row has three ✕s: Dr. Finch sat in Chair 3. That closes the (2, 4) window, so Lord Vance sat in Chair 1. Clue 3 isn’t spent yet: the whisky drinker sat farther from the fire than Chair 3, and only Chair 4 qualifies. Whisky = Chair 4. With Chairs 1 and 3 taken and Clue 4 ruling out Chair 4, General Sterling sat in Chair 2, leaving Judge Blackwood in Chair 4.

                 | C1 C2 C3 C4 | Br Po Sh Wh
-----------------+-------------+-------------
Lord Vance       |  O  X  X  X |  .  X  .  X
General Sterling |  X  O  X  X |  X  .  X  .
Dr. Finch        |  X  X  O  X |  .  X  .  X
Judge Blackwood  |  X  X  X  O |  X  .  X  .
-----------------+-------------+
Brandy           |  .  .  .  X |
Port             |  .  .  .  X |
Sherry           |  X  .  .  X |
Whisky           |  X  X  X  O |

Pass 3: Sync the Blocks

Judge Blackwood sat in Chair 4. The whisky drinker sat in Chair 4. Same person: Judge Blackwood drank whisky. His 2 × 2 box with General Sterling has one square left, so General Sterling drank the port.

Clue 5 finishes it through negative transfer. The Chair 1 member didn’t drink sherry, and Lord Vance is the Chair 1 member, so Lord Vance didn’t drink sherry. Lord Vance drank brandy; Dr. Finch drank sherry.

MemberChairGlass
Lord VanceChair 1Brandy
General SterlingChair 2Port (the poisoner)
Dr. FinchChair 3Sherry
Judge BlackwoodChair 4Whisky

Every square came from a clue or a rule. No guesses, and removing any one of the five clues leaves at least two possible answers, so each clue is doing necessary work.

Best Practices and Tips

Do:

  • Mark direct, pair, and either clues on your first pass. They clear the most squares.
  • Strike through a clue only after it has given you everything. A direct negative is spent after one ✕; an inequality or gap clue stays live until both items are placed.
  • Run a cross-block check every time you place a new ●.

Don’t:

  • Don’t skip the third ✕ on a comparison between two categories. “The letter opener was earlier than the Library” also means the letter opener wasn’t in the Library.
  • Don’t guess between two squares. A puzzle built to solve by deduction always has an uncopied mark or an unsqueezed ladder that breaks the tie.
  • Don’t carry auto-✕s across a thick border. A ● clears its own block only.

Troubleshooting Common Issues

A row or column ends up with every square crossed out. An earlier ● was wrong, usually from reversing a ladder (“earlier than” read as “later than”) or from carrying auto-✕s across a border. Check ladder clues first, since one reversed bound spreads into every block it touches. In the browser cases, Check Solution outlines any mark that contradicts the evidence. It counts against your score, so save it for when you’re stuck on a contradiction, not for testing guesses.

Every clue is marked and the board feels frozen. You have an unsynced ● or an unsynced locked pair. List every ● on the board and check that its two items have identical ✕s against every other category. Then check each two-square row: if both candidates share a ✕ somewhere else, that ✕ belongs to the row’s item too.

You keep missing ladder marks. Say the extreme case out loud: “If A was earlier than B, could A have been at 9:45? No, nobody came after 9:45.” Cross out 9:45 for A.

Conclusion

Logic grids reward a short list of mechanical moves, applied in order:

  1. A grid with C categories has C × (C − 1) ÷ 2 blocks, and each row and column inside a block holds exactly one ●.
  2. Pair and either clues clear whole lines of ✕s before you know a single match. Mark them first.
  3. Ordered clues always carry endpoint ✕s: the earlier item can’t take the last rung, and the later item can’t take the first.
  4. A comparison between two different categories also proves those two items don’t belong together.
  5. Mid-game progress comes from cross-referencing: copying ✕s through every confirmed ●.

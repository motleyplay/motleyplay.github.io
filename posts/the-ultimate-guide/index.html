The Ultimate Guide to Advanced Kakuro Tactics

Oct 8, 2026 min read

The Kakuro book said “Expert.” You believed it for the first ten minutes, while the corners filled in nicely. Now you’re looking at a block in the middle of the grid where every clue has five ways to be right, your pencil marks are three deep, and the eraser has worn a soft gray patch into the paper. You’re one bad decision away from picking a digit and hoping.

You don’t need to be better at arithmetic. Expert Kakuro grids are built to resist the tools that got you through the medium ones, so you need a few more tools. There are four of them, and each one picks up where the last runs out: magic blocks give you a starting point, intersections turn candidates into digits, the 45 rule opens blocks with no starting point, and parity and forcing chains settle the corners where everything else stalls.

Mastering Magic Blocks (Unique Sums)

Small Magic Blocks

Kakuro has two rules: digits 1 through 9, and no repeats inside an entry (the run of white cells a clue covers). Near the ends of the number range, those two rules leave only one possible set of digits for a clue. Conceptis Puzzles calls those clues magic blocks, and they’re the first thing to look for on any grid.

The small ones show up in almost every puzzle:

CellsLowHigh
23 {1,2} · 4 {1,3}16 {7,9} · 17 {8,9}
36 {1,2,3} · 7 {1,2,4}23 {6,8,9} · 24 {7,8,9}

Memorizing these isn’t about saving three seconds of addition. It changes what you see. Once “17 in two cells” reads as “8 and 9”, you stop calculating and start noticing that the 17 crosses a 16, and that the two share only the digit 9. That cell is solved before you’ve picked up the pencil.

Large Magic Blocks

Four and five cells have their own extremes: 10 {1,2,3,4}, 11 {1,2,3,5}, 29 {5,7,8,9}, and 30 {6,7,8,9} in four cells, and 15, 16, 34, and 35 in five. These rarely place a digit directly, but they cap or floor every entry that crosses them. Every cell of a 10 in four cells is 4 or lower, which wipes out the high half of any crossing pair.

One trap catches people here: “10 in four cells” is not 1 + 1 + 4 + 4 or 2 + 2 + 3 + 3. Repeats are illegal, which is exactly why 10 has only one answer.

The full table, plus a walkthrough of finding every entry point on a blank grid, is in Essential Magic Blocks.

The Power of the 45 Rule

Basic Application

1 + 2 + … + 9 = 45. Killer Sudoku players use that fact constantly, because every box must hold each digit once. Kakuro has no such boxes. An open 3×3 block can legally hold 1, 2, 3 in every row and column and total 18. Only a nine-cell entry is guaranteed to total 45.

The rule still earns its place in two ways.

Long entries are short entries inside out. An eight-cell entry skips exactly one digit, and that digit is 45 minus the clue: 38 in eight cells is missing 7. A 41 in seven cells is missing two digits that total 4, which can only be {1, 3}.

Innies and outies. Pick a region. Add the across clues of every entry that lies wholly inside it, then add the down clues the same way. If one direction covers one extra cell, the difference between the two totals is that cell. An outie sits just outside the region, caught by an entry that pokes out. An innie sits inside, missed by an entry that leaves.

Advanced Block Math

Regions don’t have to be rectangles. Any shape works as long as each entry you count is completely inside it. If the totals differ by two cells instead of one, you haven’t failed: you’ve made a new two-cell clue, and if it lands on 3, 4, 16, or 17, it’s a magic block.

The full method, including a grid where the count is the only way in, is in Mastering the 45 Rule.

Utilizing Intersection Logic

Cross-Referencing Extremes

Every entry has a ceiling and a floor. The highest digit a 14 in four cells can hold is 14 − (1 + 2 + 3) = 8. Cross that 14 with a 17 in two cells {8, 9}, and the shared cell can’t be 9, so it’s 8. One crossing decided the cell and the 17’s other cell, and it settled the 14 as {1, 2, 3, 8}.

Push the extremes too far apart and the crossing becomes impossible. A 16 {7, 9} can’t share a cell with a 10 in four cells {1, 2, 3, 4}. You’ll never see that in a valid puzzle. If you think you do, recount the cells.

Forcing Digits

Most crossings narrow instead of solving. A 17 across over a 24 down {7, 8, 9} leaves 8 or 9 in the corner. A second crossing finishes the job: if the 17’s other cell sits on a 16 down {7, 9}, that cell must be 9, and the corner must be 8.

The habit that makes this fast is simple: read the crossing clue before writing any candidates. Intersection Logic has a cheat sheet of the crossings that force digits outright.

Eliminating Guesswork with Parity

Parity Rules

When a corner stalls with two fills that both seem to work, the temptation is bifurcation: pick one and see if it breaks. Don’t. A wrong guess usually fails ten moves later, far from where you made it.

Parity is the first tool for a stuck corner. An odd clue in two cells means one odd digit and one even digit. An even clue means both odd or both even. A 4 {1, 3} and a 16 {7, 9} are made entirely of odd digits, so if a cell in one of them crosses an odd two-cell clue, the other cell of that clue must be even.

The second tool is the forcing chain: assume a candidate, follow only forced moves, and look for a “ghost” duplicate, a digit the chain would place twice in the same entry. Three or four forced links that end in a collision prove the candidate wrong. You never write the assumption down. Eliminating the Guesswork walks through a corner that looks like two valid solutions until a chain finds the ghost.

Practical Example: Solving an Expert Grid Section

Here’s a four-row section with an irregular edge. Clue cells read down\across: 17\12 holds a down 17 and an across 12.

+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######| 10\   | 16\   | 15\   |#######|#######|#######|
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|   \13 |       |       |       |#######| 12\   | 15\   |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|   \21 |       |       |       | 17\12 |       |       |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######|#######|   \24 |       |       |       |       |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######|#######|#######|   \15 |       |       |#######|
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+

Step 1: Magic blocks. Scan for the short list. The 16 down is two cells, so {7, 9}. The 17 down is two cells, so {8, 9}. Everything else has at least two possible sets. Pencil those two blocks in and look for crossings. There aren’t any that settle a digit, which is why this section is rated expert.

Step 2: The 45 rule. The top-left block is a closed 2×3 region. Its across clues cover it exactly: 13 + 21 = 34. Its down clues cover it plus one cell, because the 15 down runs one cell past the block: 10 + 16 + 15 = 41.

+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######| 10\   | 16\   | 15\   |#######|#######|#######|
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|   \13 |   ·   |  7 9  |   ·   |#######| 12\   | 15\   |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|   \21 |   ·   |  7 9  |   ·   | 17\12 |       |       |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######|#######|   \24 | OUTIE |  8 9  |       |       |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######|#######|#######|   \15 |  8 9  |       |#######|
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+

The outie is 41 − 34 = 7.

Step 3: Intersections and a ghost on the left. With 7 at its bottom, the 15 down needs 8 from its top two cells: {2, 6} or {3, 5}, since {1, 7} would repeat the 7.

Now test the 21 across. Its sets are {4, 8, 9}, {5, 7, 9}, and {6, 7, 8}. Its right-hand cell has to come from {2, 3, 5, 6}, which rules out {4, 8, 9}. Try {6, 7, 8}: the right cell is 6, the middle is 7 (the 16 down allows only 7 or 9), and the left cell is 8. Follow the chain. The 10 down puts 2 above the 8. The 15 down puts 2 above the 6. That’s two 2s in the 13 across, a ghost duplicate, so {6, 7, 8} is out.

That leaves {5, 7, 9}. The right cell is 5, so the cell above it is 3. The 10 down is even, and its bottom cell is 7 or 9, both odd, so its top cell is odd as well: 1 or 3. 3 is already in the 13 across, so it’s 1, and the cell below is 9. The 16 down finishes as 9 over 7. Check the 13 across: 1 + 9 + 3 = 13.

+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######| 10\   | 16\   | 15\   |#######|#######|#######|
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|   \13 |   1   |   9   |   3   |#######| 12\   | 15\   |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|   \21 |   9   |   7   |   5   | 17\12 |       | 7 8 9 |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######|#######|   \24 |   7   |  8 9  |       |       |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######|#######|#######|   \15 |  8 9  |       |#######|
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+

Step 4: Intersections on the right. The top-right cell sits in a 12 across ({3, 9}, {4, 8}, {5, 7}) and a 15 down ({6, 9}, {7, 8}). The digits both allow are 7, 8, and 9. The cell below it is 15 minus that, so 8, 7, or 6. The 24 across already has a 7, so it’s 8 or 6.

If it were 8, the 17 down cell in the same row would have to be 9 (the 8 is taken), and the 24 across would need 24 − 7 − 9 − 8 = 0 from its one remaining cell. Impossible. So it’s 6, the cell above is 9, and the 12 across starts with 3. The 24 across now needs 11 from its middle two cells, with the left one 8 or 9. The right one would be 3 or 2, and 3 is already in that column, so it’s 2 and the left is 9. The 17 down finishes with 8, and the 15 across needs 7 beside it. Check the 12 down: 3 + 2 + 7 = 12.

+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######| 10\   | 16\   | 15\   |#######|#######|#######|
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|   \13 |   1   |   9   |   3   |#######| 12\   | 15\   |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|   \21 |   9   |   7   |   5   | 17\12 |   3   |   9   |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######|#######|   \24 |   7   |   9   |   2   |   6   |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|#######|#######|#######|   \15 |   8   |   7   |#######|
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+

Every technique made an appearance. The magic blocks gave you candidates. The 45 rule gave you the first digit. Intersections and one short ghost chain did the rest. Not one guess.

Best Practices and Tips

Do:

  • ✓ Memorize the 2-, 3-, and 4-cell extremes.
  • ✓ Keep pencil marks small and in the same spot in each cell, so you can read them at a glance.
  • ✓ Check the crossing clue before writing candidates in any cell.

Don’t:

  • ✗ Guess. If you’re tempted, look for a ghost duplicate first.
  • ✗ Forget that digits can’t repeat inside an entry. It’s the rule that makes every technique here work.
  • ✗ Ignore large open blocks. That’s exactly where the 45 rule pays off.

Speed tips:

  • Scan the whole grid for 3, 4, 16, and 17 before touching anything else.
  • Work from the edges in. Corners and short entries constrain the most.

Troubleshooting Common Issues

Two possibilities remain for the last two cells.

  • Cause: You missed a duplicate in a crossing entry, or a clue elsewhere that tells the two fills apart.
  • Fix: Fill each option in your head and check every entry it touches, including cells just outside the pair.
  • Prevention: Check both crossing entries every time you lock in a digit.

The sums don’t add up at the end.

  • Cause: An arithmetic slip early on, or a repeated digit inside one entry.
  • Fix: Go back to the last digit you’re certain of and redo the steps after it. If the error is too old to find, start the grid fresh. Painful, but faster than unpicking a tangle.

The 45-rule count gives a digit above 9.

  • Cause: An entry in your count pokes out of the region, or you assumed a block sums to 45.
  • Fix: Count only entries completely inside the region, and compare across with down. Don’t compare against 45.

Conclusion

Key takeaways:

  1. Magic blocks are your entry points.
  2. Intersections turn those entry points into placed digits.
  3. The 45 rule opens large blocks with no magic blocks in them.
  4. Parity and short forcing chains settle the corners where everything else stalls, with no guessing.
  5. Recognizing patterns is faster than recalculating combinations.

Next steps: Practice spotting magic blocks on an easy grid until you stop reaching for the table. Then try innies and outies on a Killer Sudoku, where the regions are handed to you, before hunting for them in Kakuro.
